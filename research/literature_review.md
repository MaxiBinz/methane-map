# Methane Hunter: primary-source literature review

Research checked: 2026-10-18 (Europe/Vienna). This is a scientific background review; execution outcomes belong in the experiment log and validation report. No source below establishes that this project discovered a new emission.

## Established evidence and current data products

**Thorpe et al., 17 November 2023, Science Advances 9, eadh2391.** [Published article and author archive](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/50nm7-ayj24); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adh2391). The first EMIT results demonstrate that a surface imaging spectrometer can identify and quantify strong methane point-source emissions from orbit. Their examples span several emitting sectors and selected observing conditions. These are demonstrations, not an instrument-wide sensitivity guarantee or a complete global source inventory. This supports using published plumes as positive controls and prohibits treating an unmatched image feature as inherently novel.

**NASA LP DAAC, 2025, EMITL2BCH4ENH V002.** [Dataset DOI](https://doi.org/10.5067/EMIT/EMITL2BCH4ENH.002). V002 distributes scene-wide methane enhancement, sensor-noise uncertainty, and sensitivity in three approximately 60 m Cloud Optimized GeoTIFF layers. Enhancement units are ppm m, a column concentration-path-length enhancement; they are neither ambient mixing ratio nor emission rate. Unlike V001, this collection includes scenes without identified plume complexes. That makes V002 the appropriate starting population for a search and for catalog-negative controls. The mission samples sunlit acquisition targets from the ISS rather than providing uniform daily coverage. The 2022-09-13 through 2023-01-06 acquisition gap must not be mistaken for a lack of emissions.

**NASA EMIT SDS, user guide version 2.0; changelog dated 20 November 2025, release field still TBD.** [Current source guide](https://github.com/emit-sds/emit-sds-tgp/blob/develop/docs/EMIT_L2B_TRACE_GAS_User_Guide.md); [March/April 2025 PDF guide](https://lpdaac.usgs.gov/documents/2250/EMIT_L2B_GHG_User_Guide_V2.pdf). [Plume dataset DOI](https://doi.org/10.5067/EMIT/EMITL2BCH4PLM.002). ENH uses scene/orbit identifiers, whereas PLM uses a global plume-complex identifier. A complex can cross scene boundaries; its timestamp denotes its earliest intersecting scene. The GeoTIFFs are orthorectified in EPSG:4326 with nearest-neighbor resampling. Plume GeoJSON metadata and outlines are therefore necessary for association; a raster bounding box alone is not a plume mask. The repository guide is mutable, and its changelog is not independent proof of an archive release date.

**Brodrick et al., EMIT trace-gas ATBD version 2.0; changelog 20 November 2025, release TBD.** [Algorithm document](https://github.com/emit-sds/emit-sds-tgp/blob/develop/docs/EMIT_L2B_TRACE_GAS_ATBD.md). The adaptive matched filter estimates methane spectral contrast using scene statistics. Three reviewers vet catalog complexes conservatively; the document explicitly rejects interpreting this catalog as comprehensive. Sensor-noise uncertainty excludes surface clutter. Sensitivity correction divides enhancement by sensitivity and can amplify dim-pixel noise; the uncertainty formula already contains sensitivity normalization. Clouds, water, saturation and hot pixels are masked, but mask leakage remains possible. Temporal surface persistence, RGB context and spectral checks are part of review. Thus a threshold exceedance is a screening result, and a catalog match is documentation rather than an independent observation.

**Fahlen et al., 2024, IEEE TGRS 62, 1–10.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TGRS.2024.3440174), *Sensitivity and Uncertainty in Matched-Filter-Based Gas Detection With Imaging Spectroscopy*. This is the methodological reference named by the NASA product and ATBD for sensitivity and pixel uncertainty. Publisher access did not resolve in this review; operational conventions above were checked directly against NASA's ATBD rather than inferred from the paper title. No numerical performance claim is attributed to the uninspected publisher text.

## Detectors, segmentation and alternative retrievals

**Foote et al., 6 March 2020 preprint; IEEE TGRS publication 2020.** [Author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.02978); [journal DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TGRS.2020.2976888), *Fast and Accurate Retrieval of Methane Concentration from Imaging Spectrometer Data Using Sparsity Prior*. Sparsity and albedo correction improve matched-filter retrievals in simulated AVIRIS-NG tests. The reported improvements depend on that experiment. They do not validate arbitrary spatial thresholding of an EMIT enhancement raster or prove that a larger positive response is real gas.

**Batchu et al., MAPL-EMIT, arXiv v1 11 April 2026; v2 29 July 2026.** [Preprint v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.10094v2). The model combines hyperspectral radiance and spatial context for enhancement retrieval, segmentation and source-head localization. The authors report recovery of 84% of hand-annotated NASA complexes in a 1,084-granule benchmark and roughly 1.5 times as many plausible plumes as manual review. These are the authors' benchmark results, not measurements reproduced here. Training used physics-based synthetic injections; spectral vetting and independent real-observation checks remain essential. The accessed record is a preprint, so peer-review status is not assumed.

**Google Research MAPL repository, checked 18 October 2026; no dated release claimed.** [Source and README](https://github.com/google-research/mapl). Apache-2.0 code supports tiled inference, plume disaggregation, deduplication and spectral vetting. The documented entry point requires L1B radiance plus matching OBS and a pretrained-model path. A code license does not license third-party inputs or establish that model weights, CPU performance or all ancillary inputs are available. The initial investigation should reuse NASA retrievals and a transparent CPU screening baseline; MAPL is a higher-value subsequent verification method once its practical prerequisites are met.

## False positives, transport and quantitative limits

**Cusworth et al., 24 October 2019, Atmospheric Measurement Techniques 12, 5655–5668.** [Article](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/12/5655/2019/). Simulated and downsampled imaging-spectrometer experiments show strong dependence on surface brightness and heterogeneity. Surface/CH4 retrieval error correlations can create apparent plumes, especially over heterogeneous surfaces. Generic plume masks and wind calibrations can bias inferred rates. Bright desert terrain is a useful initial choice but does not eliminate mineral or infrastructure spectral confusers. The paper's favorable detection thresholds must not be transferred to every EMIT scene.

**Varon et al., 18 October 2018, Atmospheric Measurement Techniques 11, 5673–5686.** [Article](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/11/5673/2018/). Large-eddy simulations compare instantaneous-plume rate estimators. Integrated mass enhancement and cross-sectional flux methods outperform simple Gaussian or source-pixel approaches under the studied conditions. Rate inference needs transport information and calibration compatible with the instrument and mask; uncertain winds can dominate error, particularly at low speed. A visually inferred plume axis is not a measured wind field, and a plume maximum is not a source coordinate.

**Duren et al., 24 November 2025, Atmospheric Measurement Techniques 18, 6933–6958.** [Article](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/18/6933/2025/index.html). The Carbon Mapper system combines several platforms and targeted observing strategies. Observing-system completeness depends jointly on detectability, spatial coverage and sampling frequency. Aircraft underflights and controlled releases provide stronger external validation than comparing two catalog entries derived from the same observation. Point-source imaging does not measure all diffuse emissions. Satellite absence and catalog absence therefore require distinct interpretations.

## Access, licensing and independent comparison resources

**NASA Science Data Portal licensing policy, checked 18 October 2026.** [Policy](https://science.data.nasa.gov/about/license). Data from NASA-led missions default to CC0 unless specifically restricted; retain source attribution, product DOI, versions and granule identifiers. This policy does not override a third-party source's terms. Authentication requirements and an open license are separate issues.

**Carbon Mapper API and terms, API version 1.0.0; terms updated 13 January 2026.** [API documentation](https://api.carbonmapper.org/api/v1/docs); [terms](https://carbonmapper.org/terms); [current product guide](https://carbonmapper.org/articles/product-guide). The API offers plume/source and raster products and warns of possible uncalibrated first-light data. Terms permit mission-aligned non-commercial research, require Carbon Mapper attribution and require corresponding non-commercial attribution terms on redistribution. They are not a blanket CC0 license. Product generation and sensor provenance must be retained; EMIT entries in Carbon Mapper do not independently corroborate NASA's same EMIT acquisition.

**U.S. Greenhouse Gas Center, current V002 usage notebook, checked 18 October 2026.** [Notebook](https://us-ghg-center.github.io/ghgc-docs/user_data_notebooks/emit-ch4plume-v2_User_Notebook.html). Documents collection `emit-ch4plume-v2` through Earth.gov STAC/raster services. The shown asset points to protected LP DAAC storage. A published notebook demonstrates an access interface, not guaranteed unauthenticated full-raster access or complete current archive coverage. If raster services provide numeric values, they must be distinguished from colorized previews and nearest-native observations from resampled products.

## Realistic gaps this project can test

The established literature already covers automated methane detection, segmentation and some source localization. Original work here can consist of a reproducible bounded audit: how a transparent detector behaves on V002 positives and controls; how many unmatched enhancement components survive artifact challenges; whether recurring features follow the ground surface or change with atmospheric transport; and which validation prerequisites prevent a defensible claim. None requires training another model or promising a novel emission.
